Resume bullet points, before and after

Most weak bullets describe a job. Strong bullets describe what you did in it and what changed. Here are eight rewrites and the reasoning behind each one.

Reviewed by RoastMyResume · Published September 26, 2026

How to rewrite a resume bullet point

  1. Start with a specific verb for what you did: built, cut, negotiated, trained, rewrote.
  2. Name the object: the system, process, account, or team you worked on.
  3. Add scope: how many, how much, how often, or for whom.
  4. End with the result: what changed because of the work, measured if you can defend the number.

Keep each bullet to one or two lines. If a result is unknown, a clear scope marker is better than an invented percentage.

Eight rewrites

Customer support

Before: “Responsible for answering customer tickets.”

After: “Resolved 60+ billing and login tickets a day and wrote 14 help-center articles that cut repeat contacts on the top issue by 22%.”

Why it works: “Responsible for” states a duty. The rewrite shows volume, the kind of problems, and one improvement the candidate caused.

Software engineer

Before: “Worked on the backend using Python and AWS.”

After: “Moved the order-export job from a nightly cron script to an SQS-driven Python worker, cutting export delays from 12 hours to under 5 minutes.”

Why it works: Tools belong inside the accomplishment, not in place of it. The reader learns what was built and why it mattered.

Sales

Before: “Exceeded sales targets consistently.”

After: “Closed $1.3M in new mid-market contracts in 2025, 118% of quota, including the region's largest logistics account.”

Why it works: “Consistently” is a claim. A figure, a quota comparison, and one notable win are evidence.

Project manager

Before: “Managed multiple projects from start to finish.”

After: “Ran 6 concurrent office build-outs with budgets up to $400K, delivering all six on schedule and two under budget.”

Why it works: “Multiple projects” could mean anything. The number, type, and budget ceiling establish the level of responsibility.

Retail or hospitality

Before: “Trained new employees.”

After: “Trained 25 new hires on POS and opening procedures; wrote the store's first one-page shift checklist, later adopted by 3 nearby locations.”

Why it works: Frontline roles have scale and initiative too. Count the people and show what outlasted your shift.

Marketing

Before: “Helped with social media and email campaigns.”

After: “Wrote and scheduled the weekly customer newsletter for 40K subscribers; A/B-tested subject lines to lift open rate from 21% to 27%.”

Why it works: “Helped with” hides your part. Claim the piece you owned, even if the campaign was a team effort.

Recent graduate

Before: “Completed a capstone project on data analysis.”

After: “Analyzed 3 years of city 311 complaint data in Python and presented a response-time dashboard to the city's operations team.”

Why it works: Without much work history, the project is the evidence. Give it a data set, a method, and an audience.

Administrative

Before: “Handled scheduling and office tasks.”

After: “Managed calendars and travel for 4 executives, including a 120-person offsite, and replaced paper expense forms with a shared tracker.”

Why it works: Specific people, events, and one process improvement turn “office tasks” into a picture of the job.

These examples are fictional. Replace every number, tool, and result with information you can defend in an interview.

What if you don't have a number?

Do not invent one. Recruiters ask about numbers, and a made-up figure is worse than none. Instead, look for scope you can verify: how many people, customers, locations, records, or projects; how often; or how large a budget. Those are usually in an old email, report, or calendar.

When we generate rewrites in Resume Boost, any number the AI cannot know is left in brackets, like “trained [N] new hires”, so you fill it in from your own records or delete it.

Weak openings to replace

  • “Responsible for…” describes the job ad, not your work
  • “Helped with…” or “Assisted in…” hides what you actually did
  • “Worked on…” says nothing about the outcome
  • “Successfully…” or “Effectively…” adds an adjective instead of evidence
  • A list of tools with no task attached

Put the strongest bullet first

Order bullets under each role by relevance to the job you want, not by how often you did the task. If the posting stresses leadership, the bullet about training new hires moves above the one about daily tickets. The ATS guide covers how to mirror a posting's language without keyword stuffing.
