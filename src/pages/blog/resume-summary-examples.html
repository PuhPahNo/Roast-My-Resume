Opening section

A resume summary should narrow the reader's understanding

State the role, the specialty, and the strongest proof relevant to this application. Skip adjectives that could describe anyone.

Reviewed by RoastMyResume · Updated August 30, 2026

First decide whether you need one

A summary is useful when it helps connect a complicated background to the target: a career change, broad leadership record, specialized technical focus, or return after a break. It can also give an experienced candidate a quick frame for the achievements below.

If you are early in your career and the summary would only repeat your degree and desired title, use that space for projects, skills, or experience instead.

A flexible three-part structure

  1. Role and level: what you do and, when useful, the depth of experience.
  2. Specialty: the problems, customers, environment, or tools most relevant to the target.
  3. Proof: one defensible result, scale marker, or distinctive qualification.

Two compact sentences are often enough. The structure is a starting point, not a requirement to force three facts into every summary.

Five summary examples

Mid-career marketing analyst

“Marketing analyst with 5 years measuring paid acquisition for subscription businesses. Built reporting used to reallocate a $2.4M annual media budget and reduce cost per trial by 18%.”

Why it works: The audience can see the role, environment, scope, and one result without a list of personality traits.

Operations leader

“Multi-site operations director overseeing 11 locations and 140 employees across the Northeast. Specializes in labor planning and service recovery; most recently brought on-time fulfillment from 91% to 97%.”

Why it works: Team and location scale establish level before the reader reaches the work history.

Career changer

“Former classroom teacher moving into learning design after 8 years building standards-aligned instruction and training new faculty. Completed an Articulate Storyline certificate and developed three scenario-based portfolio modules.”

Why it works: The transition is direct, while the transferable work and recent proof carry the argument.

Recent graduate with projects

“Computer science graduate focused on data engineering, with projects in Python, SQL, and Airflow. Built a pipeline that processed 1.8M public transit records and documented data-quality checks in a public repository.”

Why it works: The project supplies evidence the candidate cannot yet get from a long employment history.

Returning after a career break

“Customer-success manager returning after a two-year caregiving break, with prior experience managing a $3.1M renewal portfolio for B2B software accounts. Refreshed Salesforce administration skills through a 2026 certification.”

Why it works: It states the break without private detail, then centers relevant prior scope and current preparation.

These examples are fictional. Replace every role, number, credential, and result with information you can defend.

Delete these before adding another sentence

  • “Hardworking,” “motivated,” “dynamic,” or “results-driven” without evidence
  • A goal so broad it fits every opening
  • A list of tools already easy to find in the skills section
  • Claims such as “excellent communicator” that the experience never demonstrates
  • Third-person biography language

Tailor by emphasis, not invention

You do not need an entirely different identity for each application. Choose the true specialty and result that best match the posting, then make sure the work history supports the claim. If it does not, fix the summary or remove it.
